# Preface: How to Read This Book

*Some of what follows is already running on hardware we can point to. Some of it is a drawing on a napkin. This page is about telling you which is which — every single time.*

**This is a book with a thesis, a set of live prototypes, a set of deliberate fictions, and a rule that they never get to blur together.**

The thesis is simple enough to say in one breath: *seed capital is changing substrate.* For a certain kind of founder — the solo open-source developer with an idea and a model API bill — the scarce input is no longer money to hire a team. It is **compute and inference.** And there is a funding primitive, the **revnet**, that fits this founder better than any seed round ever fit anyone. The whole book is the long version of that sentence, worked out in mechanics, in numbers, and in five worked examples.

But a thesis this clean is exactly the kind of thing that gets oversold, so before anything else, three commitments to you.

## Commitment one: we will always tell you what is real

This repository is a working notebook, and it contains two very different kinds of thing. Some of the projects here are **LIVE** — a prototype actually exists, on actual machines, and we will say so and tell you precisely what it does and does not yet do. The digital-immortality network behind the token called $WAKE is the main one: there really is a small constellation of avatars posting to a commons tonight. Others are **HYPOTHETICAL** — designed sample projects that exist to make a mechanism legible. The developer who hires an AI agent as his own boss ($SERF), the Tennessee construction company tokenizing its profit ($HARD), the centralized firm that still chooses a revnet ($CITE) — those are fictions in service of an argument. Useful fictions, carefully built, but fictions.

Every time one of them appears, it wears a label: **[LIVE]** or **[HYPOTHETICAL]**. If we ever let a napkin drawing read like a shipped product, we have failed at the one job this preface exists to promise. Hold us to it.

## Commitment two: this is not legal or financial advice

Tokens that represent claims on revenue, profit, or a floor of reserves live squarely in territory governed by securities law — and that territory is not this book's subject. Where the law is decisive, we will say so plainly, name the question, and stop, deferring to actual counsel. You will notice whole categories of "but is that legal?" that we raise and then refuse to answer. That refusal is deliberate. *Do not, under any circumstances, read this book as giving legal advice or attorney approval.* It reasons about economics, mechanism design, and security — the parts an engineer can check — and it leaves the law to lawyers.

## Commitment three: every good-sounding claim owes you the catch

The failure mode of every piece of writing about tokens is the same: a mechanism that sounds like free money, described in the register of a sales pitch. This book has a house rule against it. **Any sentence that could read as hype has to be grounded, quantified, or caveated in the same breath.** When we tell you a token's floor can rise on its own, we owe you — immediately, not in a footnote three chapters later — the exact condition under which that is true and the exact way it breaks when the condition fails. When something looks too good, the catch is the interesting part, and you will find it next to the claim, not hidden from it.

If you are a skeptic, you are the reader we are writing for. The book has an entire chapter (Part IV) that does nothing but attack its own thesis — *isn't this just a ponzi, just equity in a costume, just a Kickstarter with extra steps?* — and answers each with the strongest version of the objection, then concedes the part where the skeptic is right. If those answers don't hold, the thesis doesn't either, and you should be able to tell.

## How the book is built

It moves in five parts. **Part I** makes the case that compute has become the new seed capital and teaches you what a revnet is from zero. **Part II** is the mechanics — including the turn the whole book pivots on, when the treasury stops sitting idle and starts producing the very compute the product burns. **Part III** walks the five examples, one chapter each. **Part IV** is the honest accounting of risk. **Part V** is a playbook: how a single developer could actually do this, step by step.

You can read it straight through, or you can skip to the example that matches your situation and backfill the mechanics from the glossary. Either way, the labels travel with you.

*Read it like a skeptic. We built it to survive that.*
